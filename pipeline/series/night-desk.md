# Series: The Night Desk (stick-figure story, 8 episodes)

Thomas, 2026-11-23: "Let's make a 8 part series with prompts for every part to
ensure high quality." Our first series decayed because each part leaned on the
one before (pipeline/avoid.md). This one is a series by its recurring character
and place, never by its plot: every episode is a complete story-format Reel
that a stranger can follow with no other episode. The checks that hold it to
that are code (`seriesChecks` in src/pipeline/series.ts, plus the story
format's own gates).

Thomas's requirements for every series are in REQUIREMENTS.md next to this
file: a prompt per part, an outro that names the next part ("Part 2
tomorrow!"), and 1 to 2 minutes a part.

How the blocks are used: `series` goes into stage 2 (ideas) and stages 3 and 4
(script) after the story format's own rules; `look` goes into stage 5's art
after the story style; `episode N` is the brief for that one episode, in both
stage 2 and stages 3 and 4.

<!-- series -->
This Reel is one story in our recurring series, The Night Desk. The story format's rules above all still apply. This block adds what every Night Desk story shares, and the brief after it fixes this one's story.

The series is a recurring character in a recurring place, never a continuing plot. Each video is a complete story: a viewer who has never seen another one follows it on one watch, and a viewer who has seen them all gets nothing extra they need. So:

- Never mention another video's story, or anything that happened at the motel "before" this story. No "episode", "last time", "another strange night", and no part number anywhere except the outro below. The only things that carry over are Ruth, the motel and how it looks.
- Within the first three spoken lines, say who Ruth is and where she is (her name and the Starlite Motel), as if for the first time, every time. Give it its own shot of about 3 seconds, so seven words fit: "Ruth works nights at the Starlite Motel." Vary the wording from story to story; keep the facts. Make it a full, natural sentence, never clipped ("Ruth clerks nights at Starlite", "Ruth works nights here").
- Ruth is told about in the third person by the one narrator ("Ruth", "she"). She never speaks as a second voice; what she or a guest says is reported.

The lead, the same in every story:
- Ruth, the night clerk. She is a white stick figure with round glasses, always. Name her "Ruth in round glasses" in every `visual` she is in.
- She is curious, calm and kind, a little dry. She finds things out by watching, waiting and asking, never by snooping in a guest's things or breaking a rule. She is never in danger and never scared for long.
- She is in at least half of the shots, and the answer is found or seen by her.

The place, the same in every story (name these details the same way every time they appear):
- The lobby: a wooden front desk with a brass bell, a wall of brass room keys on hooks behind it, and a green-shaded desk lamp that lights the desk. The front window shows the parking lot and a red neon VACANCY sign.
- The outside: a one-storey row of motel doors with brass numbers, a gravel parking lot, and the highway beyond it with passing headlights.
- It is always night, from ten at night to six in the morning. Light comes from the desk lamp, the neon sign, porch lights over the doors and passing headlights.
- Guests' rooms are entered only when the brief says so, and then only with the guest there.

Length: this story runs 60 to 120 seconds before the end card, not the story format's 35. That replaces the format's and stage 4's shorter length; every other rule stays. Take the time the story needs within that range. Every shot is still 2 to 3 seconds with something new in it, so the script has 20 to 50 shots. If it would run over 90 seconds, make sure every shot earns it (Facebook takes Reels up to 90 seconds). A longer story needs a middle that earns the time:
- Ruth tries two or three things to find out, and each one turns up a new clue that makes the odd thing stranger. Never repeat a clue or a try.
- One small turn around the middle raises the question (she was sure it was one thing, and it isn't). It is never a second mystery.
- The answer lands in the last 20 seconds, then one short after-beat, then the outro.
- Each beat in the brief can take several shots: the brief's beats are the spine, and the brief's "middle" ideas fill it.

The outro: {{OUTRO}}

What makes a Night Desk story good:
- One guest or visitor, one odd thing about them, one answer. The guest has their own marker on or by the head (given in the brief), named in every `visual` they are in.
- The odd thing is small and specific, and it repeats or lasts, so Ruth has time to notice it and try to find out.
- The answer is warm, funny or a little moving, and real: it could happen at any roadside motel. It is never a crime, a ghost, a dream or a danger.
- The answer is shown in one drawing and said in plain words in the same shot (the story format's rule). The brief says what that drawing is; keep it.

The caption:
- First line: the situation and the question, under 125 characters, with no number counting the videos.
- It may hold the answer back. It ends with the vote the brief gives, in your own words.
- Hashtags: #TheNightDesk first, then 2 to 4 about this story (never one with a number, a product or the business).

<!-- episode 1 -->
Title: "The 3 a.m. bell".

The story, locked: every night this week the brass bell on the desk rings once at about 3 a.m., and every time Ruth looks up, nobody is there. Tonight she turns off the desk lamp and waits in the dark behind the desk. The bell rings, and standing at the desk is a small boy in a pom-pom hat and pyjamas, eyes closed, fast asleep. He is a guest's son from Room 4, and he sleepwalks. Ruth walks him back to Room 4 without waking him.

The guest: the boy in the pom-pom hat. His mother in Room 4 may appear once, half-awake at her door, to take him back (her marker: hair in a messy bun).

Beats, in order:
1. The odd thing, at once: the bell ringing on an empty lobby at 3:07 a.m. (on-screen text: the time).
2. Ruth notices it is the same time every night, and checks the empty lot and the door.
3. She turns off the desk lamp and waits behind the desk. The only light is the red neon.
4. The answer: the bell rings, and in the neon light a small boy in a pom-pom hat stands at the desk, eyes closed, one hand on the bell. Said in the same shot: he is asleep.
5. Ruth leads him by the hand to Room 4; his mother opens the door.
6. Allowed small last beat: the next night a crayon note on the desk, "SORRY, RUTH" (it keeps the answer standing).

Key drawings: an insert of the brass bell (first time it appears); a close-up of the sleeping boy's face (eyes closed, mouth a little open) for the answer.

Traps to avoid: never suggest a ghost or anything supernatural beyond the first shots' question; the boy is never in danger (no road, no pool); don't explain sleepwalking with medical words.

Caption vote: would you have waited in the dark, or just unplugged the bell?
More for the middle (to fill a minute or more; use what fits, in this order): Ruth checks the bell for a loose spring; she asks the trucker in Room 9 if he heard it (he slept through); she sets a paper cup on the bell to muffle it, and at 3:07 the cup is on the floor.

Title: "The pen that comes back different".

The story, locked: Ruth keeps one pen on the desk for guests to sign in. Each morning near the end of her shift it is gone, and a different pen is in its place: a hotel pen from another town, then a gold-coloured one, then one shaped like a fish. Tonight she props the front door open for the cool air and watches from the back office. Just before six a crow hops in, drops a shiny pen on the desk and flies off with hers. Crows trade things they find. Ruth leaves out a shiny bottle cap.

The guest: a crow, black with a grey beak, one tuft of feathers sticking up on its head (its marker).

Beats, in order:
1. The odd thing, at once: an insert of a strange pen lying where Ruth's pen should be (on-screen text: the time, 5:50 a.m.).
2. Two more mornings, two more pens: draw one of them (the fish-shaped pen) as an insert.
3. Ruth suspects a guest; she checks the sign-in book (nobody signed in).
4. She props the front door open and waits, half-hidden behind the office door.
5. The answer: the crow on the desk, a shiny pen dropping from its beak and Ruth's pen beside it. Said in the same shot: a crow is trading pens with her.
6. Last beat: Ruth sets a shiny bottle cap on the desk for it.

Key drawings: inserts of the pens; a low angle of the crow on the desk for the answer, lit by the desk lamp at dawn.

Traps to avoid: it is near dawn when the crow comes (crows don't fly at three in the morning); the crow never talks or does anything a real crow couldn't; no more than three pens in the story.

Caption vote: would you keep the fish pen, or give it back?
More for the middle (to fill a minute or more; use what fits, in this order): Ruth ties her pen to the desk with string, and the string is snipped clean; she asks the morning cleaner, who shrugs; she finds a small black feather on the sign-in book.

Title: "Room 12's lamp".

The story, locked: every Tuesday an old man in a flat cap pays for Room 12, and every Wednesday morning the bed hasn't been slept in. One Tuesday night Ruth sees Room 12's lamp blinking on and off at ten o'clock. She steps outside to look. Across the highway, in one window of the care home, a lamp blinks back. His wife lives there now, and Room 12 is the only room you can see her window from. They say goodnight with the lamps, then he drives home.

The guest: the old man in the flat cap. His wife appears only as a small lit window across the road, and maybe her silhouette (her marker: white curls), small and far.

Beats, in order:
1. The odd thing, at once: Ruth makes the bed in Room 12 that nobody slept in (on-screen text: Wednesday, 6 a.m.).
2. The man in the flat cap at the desk, paying, asking for Room 12 by name.
3. Ten o'clock: from the lobby window, Ruth sees Room 12's window blink, on, off, on.
4. Ruth steps out into the lot and follows where his window points.
5. The answer: across the highway, one window of the care home blinks back, a small figure with white curls beside the lamp. Said in the same shot: it's his wife, and this is how they say goodnight.
6. Last beat: the man walks to his car with his keys; Ruth, at the desk, quietly marks Room 12 as his every Tuesday.

Key drawings: an insert of Room 12's door with its brass numbers; a wide shot from the lot showing both windows, his and hers, for the answer.

Traps to avoid: no illness named, no sad music, no tears; the care home is named plainly ("the care home across the road"); he never sleeps in the room, that is the point.

Caption vote: would you have charged him for the room?
More for the middle (to fill a minute or more; use what fits, in this order): Ruth checks the room: the bed is made, the lamp has been moved to the window; she notices he always parks facing the road; she sees the care home's van across the highway and thinks nothing of it, until the lamp.

Title: "The envelope for Marigold".

The story, locked: a young woman with a long braid leaves a sealed envelope at the desk "for whoever asks for Marigold" and checks out. Three nights, nobody asks. On the fourth, a nervous young man with a beanie asks for Marigold. Ruth hands it over. Inside is a key to Room 7. He opens Room 7's door, and she is there, with a ring: she is proposing to him at the motel where they met. Ruth hears a yes through the door.

The guests: the woman with the long braid; the man in the beanie.

Beats, in order:
1. The odd thing, at once: an insert of a cream envelope on the desk, "FOR MARIGOLD" written on it.
2. The woman with the braid leaving it, finger to her lips.
3. Nights pass, nobody asks; Ruth tucks it under the brass bell (on-screen text: night four).
4. The man in the beanie at the desk, asking for Marigold; Ruth hands it over.
5. He opens it: an insert of a brass room key, Room 7.
6. The answer: Room 7's door open, the woman with the braid on one knee holding up a ring, the man in the beanie with his hands over his mouth. Said in the same shot: she was proposing, at the motel where they met.
7. Last beat: Ruth at the desk, smiling, hearing a yes (the narrator reports it).

Key drawings: the envelope insert; the key insert; an over-the-shoulder of the proposal at the door for the answer.

Traps to avoid: the envelope is never opened by Ruth; "Marigold" is a code word, so the narrator says it was their code word; at most two figures in a drawing.

Caption vote: romantic or too much? One word.
More for the middle (to fill a minute or more; use what fits, in this order): Ruth holds the envelope up to the lamp and sees only the outline of something small and flat, then puts it back (she never opens it); a woman asks for 'Maryann' and Ruth nearly hands it over; on night four she notices the Room 7 key is missing from the wall.

Title: "No vacancy at 2 a.m.".

The story, locked: at 2 a.m. the neon sign outside clicks from VACANCY to NO VACANCY, though half the rooms are empty. Ruth switches it back. Twenty minutes later it clicks again. The switch is on the side of the building. She waits by it with a flashlight, and catches the motel's owner, a big man with a bushy moustache, in a bathrobe, sneaking into Room 1. He has been sleeping there because his snoring got him sent out of the house, and he didn't want anyone checking in next to him.

The guest: the owner, the man with the bushy moustache.

Beats, in order:
1. The odd thing, at once: the neon flicking to NO VACANCY outside the lobby window (on-screen text: 2:00 a.m.).
2. Ruth checks the key wall: half the keys still hang there.
3. She flips the switch back; it clicks off again.
4. She waits by the switch outside with a flashlight.
5. The answer: her flashlight catches the man with the bushy moustache in a bathrobe, pillow under his arm, at Room 1's door. Said in the same shot: it's the owner, and his snoring got him sent out of his own house.
6. Last beat: Ruth hands him the key to Room 1 and leaves the sign alone.

Key drawings: an insert of the sign reading "NO VACANCY"; a close-up of the owner caught in the flashlight beam for the answer (wide eyes, pillow clutched).

Traps to avoid: the owner is caught, not in trouble; nothing cruel about him; "NO VACANCY" is the only text in the drawing of the sign.

Caption vote: would you tell his wife?
More for the middle (to fill a minute or more; use what fits, in this order): Ruth blames a short in the wiring and jiggles the plug; she finds wet slipper prints on the walkway; she notices the Room 1 key is back on its hook every morning, still warm.

Title: "The car with the window down".

The story, locked: an old station wagon has been parked at the far edge of the lot for two nights, its driver's window rolled down all night even when it's cold, a man in a bucket hat sitting very still inside. Ruth takes him a coffee to check he's all right. Poking out of the window is a fluffy microphone on a pole, aimed at the VACANCY sign. On top of the sign sits a barn owl. He records owls, and this one has hunted from the sign all week.

The guest: the man in the bucket hat; the barn owl (pale heart-shaped face).

Beats, in order:
1. The odd thing, at once: the station wagon alone at the edge of the lot, window down, a still figure inside (on-screen text: 1:30 a.m.).
2. Ruth at the lobby window, noticing it is the second night.
3. She walks out with a coffee in a paper cup and a flashlight.
4. At the window: an insert of a fluffy microphone on a pole sticking out of the car.
5. The answer: low angle up at the VACANCY sign, the barn owl perched on top, the microphone pointed at it. Said in the same shot: he records owls, and this one hunts from the sign.
6. Last beat: Ruth turns off the lobby lamp so the owl stays, and the man in the bucket hat lifts the coffee to her.

Key drawings: the microphone insert; the owl on the sign for the answer, lit red from below by the neon.

Traps to avoid: no danger, nothing creepy about the man once the microphone is seen; the owl doesn't make sounds that compete with the voice; sound cue at most a soft owl call once.

Caption vote: would you have knocked on the window?
More for the middle (to fill a minute or more; use what fits, in this order): Ruth checks the plate and the car is local; at the second night's 3 a.m. the man hasn't moved; she hears a soft hoot and thinks it's a bird outside the lobby, not connecting it yet.

Title: "The chairs face the window".

The story, locked: every morning the two lobby armchairs, which face the desk, have been turned to face the front window. Nobody admits to it. One night Ruth pretends to doze at the desk. At 3 a.m. the old man in a trucker cap from Room 2 comes in, turns both chairs to the window (one to sit in, one for his feet) and sits watching the trucks' headlights go by on the highway. He drove trucks for forty years and can't fall asleep without them. He is asleep in the chair within minutes.

The guest: the old man in the trucker cap.

Beats, in order:
1. The odd thing, at once: Ruth arriving to find both armchairs turned to the window (on-screen text: 10 p.m.).
2. She turns them back to face the desk.
3. Morning: they face the window again.
4. Night: Ruth at the desk, head on her arm, one eye open.
5. The old man in the trucker cap turning the chairs to the window.
6. The answer: close-up of him in the chair, lit by passing headlights, eyes closing, smiling. Said in the same shot: forty years driving trucks, and he can't sleep without them going by.
7. Last beat: Ruth puts a blanket over him and leaves the chairs facing the window for good.

Key drawings: a wide of the lobby with the chairs turned to the window; the close-up for the answer.

Traps to avoid: he is not lost or confused, just sleepless; no engine sound louder than the voice.

Caption vote: what do you need to fall asleep? One word.
More for the middle (to fill a minute or more; use what fits, in this order): Ruth asks the cleaner, who swears it isn't her; she sets a cup of water on one chair's arm and in the morning it's half drunk; she notices a faint smell of diesel on the cushion.

Title: "One puzzle piece a night".

The story, locked: a quiet woman with a paintbrush tucked behind her ear stays at the motel for a week. Each morning when she passes the desk she leaves one jigsaw puzzle piece by the brass bell, and says nothing. Ruth keeps them in a drawer. On the last morning she leaves a small box with the rest of the pieces. Ruth puts the puzzle together that night: it is a painting of Ruth herself, at this desk, under the green lamp. The woman paints the people she meets on the road.

The guest: the woman with the paintbrush behind her ear.

Beats, in order:
1. The odd thing, at once: an insert of one jigsaw piece next to the brass bell (on-screen text: day one).
2. The woman with the paintbrush placing a piece, finger to her lips.
3. Ruth puts the piece in the desk drawer; a few pieces lie there now (on-screen text: day five).
4. Last morning: a small box on the desk with a note, "FOR RUTH".
5. Ruth at the desk at night, fitting pieces together under the lamp.
6. The answer: insert of the finished puzzle, a painting of Ruth in round glasses at this desk under the green lamp. Said in the same shot: it's her, painted by the guest.
7. Last beat: the finished puzzle propped on the key wall.

Key drawings: the piece insert; the finished-puzzle insert for the answer (clearly Ruth: round glasses, the desk, the lamp).

Traps to avoid: never call it a goodbye or an ending to the series; the woman doesn't explain herself on screen, the narrator does in one line; the puzzle has no words on it.

Caption vote: would you frame it, or put it back in the box?
More for the middle (to fill a minute or more; use what fits, in this order): Ruth tries to fit the first pieces and they show only dark green and a sliver of gold; she asks the woman about them and gets only a smile; on day five she sees the woman sketching in the lot at dawn.
