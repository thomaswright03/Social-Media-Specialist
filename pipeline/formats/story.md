# Format: stick-figure story (entertainment)

A standalone animated story told by a narrator, like the stick-figure story
channels that win in our niche (stage 1). It never sells.

<!-- ideas -->
Format: a stick-figure story. Entertainment: it never names a product or the business.
What wins: an ordinary person in one strange, specific situation that the viewer can picture themselves in ("Imagine you..."), one mystery, and an ending that answers it with a small surprise. Night shifts, neighbours, kids' businesses, pets, odd jobs, family secrets, small towns. Grounded and a little eerie or funny, never gore.
Every pitch is a story a stranger follows on one watch in 35 seconds: one or two characters, one or two places, one question set up at the start and answered at the end.

<!-- script -->
Format: a stick-figure story. Entertainment: never name a product or the business.
- A narrator tells it out loud from the first second (the first shot has a line) and speaks in nearly every shot. With sound on, the voice alone says who this is, where they are and what happens. First person ("I") or a named character; say who "I" or "he" is the first time.
- 10 to 14 shots, 2 to 3 seconds each. Each shot's visual is ONE clear action a single drawing shows mid-move, with at most two characters: she hands him the cup, he slams the sign down, the cup tips over. Never two actions in one shot. When two characters are in the story, show them doing things to each other (giving, taking, pointing, arguing face to face), not standing alone.
- Shoot it like a film. Each shot names its camera: wide, medium, close-up, over-the-shoulder, low angle, high angle or insert (a big close look at a prop). Never the same camera twice in a row, and use at least 3 kinds: open wide on the place, cut in close on faces for the feelings, an insert for the thing that matters, over-the-shoulder for two people talking.
- Name the real objects in the visual (a folding table with a striped awning, a glass pitcher, a hand-written price sign), so the art can draw them recognisably.
- One question, one answer: a single mystery set up at the start and answered by the payoff. At most one small twist at the very end, only if it doesn't confuse the answer.
- The caption ends with an audience vote ("Would you have opened it? Tell me in the comments") and 3 to 5 hashtags.
<!-- /script -->

<!-- style -->
A moody flat-vector film still, like the best stick-figure story channels. Characters are white stick figures (stroke #f5f5f5, stroke-width 12 to 18, round caps and joins, simple expressive faces: eyes, brows and a mouth that show the feeling). Places are drawn in full, edge to edge, with depth: a floor or ground plane in perspective, walls or sky behind, 2 or 3 layers where far things are smaller and darker, and real detail that says where we are (bricks, a fence, a tree, a doorway, a car). Light comes from something in the scene (a lamp, a window, the sun, a streetlight) as soft radial gradients with a pool of light on the ground and a soft shadow under each character; the rest falls off into dark (#0e1117 to #3a4150). One accent, lime #a3e635, used small, for what matters (the key prop, a glow); red #ef4444 only for danger. Nothing big and flat lime.
