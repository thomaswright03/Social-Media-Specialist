# Format: stick-figure story (entertainment)

A standalone animated story told by a narrator, like the stick-figure story
channels that win in our niche (stage 1). It never sells.

<!-- ideas -->
Format: a stick-figure story. It is entertainment: it never names a product or the business, and never asks anyone to buy anything.

What you are pitching: a story a stranger scrolling past can follow on one watch, in about 35 seconds, told by one narrator over still drawings of stick figures. That is very little room. There is no time to explain a world, a history or a set of rules, and one drawing has to make the place recognisable at a glance. So the pitches that win share a shape:

- An ordinary person, someone the viewer could be or could know. Who they are fits in three or four words: a night cleaner, a new neighbour, a kid with a paper round.
- A familiar place that needs no explaining. The viewer already knows what it looks like and what normally happens there.
- One thing that is off: small, specific and strange. Not "something weird was going on" but exactly what, such as the same shoe on the doorstep every morning.
- One question that follows from it, which the viewer asks without being told to.
- One answer, shown and said before the video ends, with a small surprise: the viewer did not guess it, and it makes sense the moment they hear it.

Tone: grounded, and a little eerie or a little funny. Grounded means it could happen on a real street to a real person. Never gore, and nothing a viewer would have to look away from.

Territory that tends to work: night shifts, neighbours, kids' businesses, pets, odd jobs, family secrets, small towns. These show the kind of thing; they are not the full list. Anywhere ordinary life has a routine that one odd detail can break is fair game, and a fresh setting beats a familiar one done again.

Test every pitch before you offer it:

1. Imagine-you test. Put it to the viewer as "Imagine you..." and they picture themselves there at once. (This tests the pitch. The video itself is narrated in first person or about a named character.)
2. Two-sentence test. Sentence one is the situation and the question; sentence two is the answer. If it needs a third sentence, it is too big.
3. One or two characters, one or two places, one stretch of time told in order.
4. The answer can be drawn. One still picture shows it: a thing found, a person revealed, what was behind the door. An answer that is only an idea or a feeling can't be shown.
5. It is finished. The question raised at the start is answered in this video, and nothing depends on another video.

Pitches that fail, and why:

- Two mysteries, or a twist that changes what the whole story meant. A stranger can't retell it after one watch.
- An answer that cancels the story: it was a dream, or it is never explained. The viewer was promised an answer.
- A story that lives in conversation. A narrator over still drawings can report a line or two, not an argument.
- Crowds, chases, fights, or anything that only reads in motion. Each shot is one still drawing of one action.
- A situation that needs backstory before it becomes strange.

<!-- script -->
Format: a stick-figure story. It is entertainment: no field, including the caption and the hashtags, names a product or the business, or asks anyone to buy anything.

How this video is made, which is the reason for every rule below: one narrator's voice plays over a sequence of still drawings, one drawing per shot. The characters are white stick figures in a drawn place. The artist draws each shot from its `visual` and `camera`, and may see that shot alone.

The examples in these rules show the size and kind of thing meant. Do not reuse their objects, places or wording in a script.

The story:

- One question, one answer. The opening sets the question. In the middle the character tries to find out, and each thing they try or notice makes it stranger. The payoff answers it.
- The answer is shown in a drawing and said in plain words by the narrator, in the same shot. Don't leave the viewer to work it out.
- At most one small twist, in the last shot, and only if it leaves the answer standing. If it would make a viewer doubt the answer, cut it.
- Grounded, and a little eerie or a little funny. Never gore, and nothing a viewer would have to look away from.

The narrator:

- One voice tells everything. Every spoken word is the narrator's. What a character says is reported by the narrator ("He told me to stop asking"), never performed as a second voice.
- First person ("I") or about a named character. The first time the narrator says "I", "he" or "she", the same line says who that is and where they are: "I was the night cleaner at a bowling alley." If the opening has already done this, don't do it again.
- A line in the first shot, and in at least three of every four shots. A shot is silent only when the silence is the point, such as the moment before the answer.
- Ears-only test: someone listening without looking learns who this is, where they are, what happens and what the answer was.
- A line and its drawing are the same moment. The drawing shows what the line says, and the line may add what a drawing can't: a thought, a reason, a time. Never narrate one thing over a drawing of another.
- It sounds like a person telling a friend what happened: short plain words, contractions, and one tense all the way through. No "we see", and no mention of the camera.

The drawings (`visual`):

- One action per shot, caught mid-move, that a single still drawing can show: she slides the envelope under the door; he lifts the tarp off the boat; the dog drops a glove on the step. Never two actions in a shot. If describing it needs "and then", it is two shots.
- A still can't show something happening over time. Pick the instant that tells it: the hand on the handle, or the door already open, never "the door slowly opens".
- At most two characters in a drawing. When the story has two, show them doing something to each other (handing over, taking, pointing, arguing face to face), not standing side by side.
- Stick figures look alike. Give each character one marker that lasts the whole video (a cap, a ponytail, a scarf, a child's height), and name the character by it in every `visual`: "the boy in the cap".
- Every `visual` stands on its own. Write it for an artist who sees only this shot: who is in it, where they are, what they are doing, with what, and what their face shows. Never "same as before", "he does it again" or "the room".
- Name the real objects, specifically enough to draw them recognisably: not "a counter" but "a glass counter with a brass till"; not "a light" but "a bare bulb on a cord".
- Each place has two or three fixed details, named the same way in every shot set there, so the viewer knows it is still the same place.
- Say where the light comes from: a desk lamp, a streetlight through the blinds, an open fridge. The art is dark and moody, and lights each scene from something inside it.
- Feelings are faces and bodies: raised brows, an open mouth, a step back, dropped shoulders. Never a feeling as a label.
- Words inside the drawing (a sign, a note, a phone screen): give the exact words in quotes, three words or fewer, in a close-up or an insert so they can be read.

The camera:

- `camera` is exactly one of: wide, medium, close-up, over-the-shoulder, low angle, high angle, insert.
- wide: the whole place and where people stand in it. Use it for the first shot in each place.
- medium: one or two figures from the knees up, doing something.
- close-up: a face, for the feeling.
- over-the-shoulder: two people facing each other.
- low angle: looking up at something that looms.
- high angle: looking down on someone small, alone or found out.
- insert: the object that matters, filling the frame.
- Change the camera on every cut, and use at least three kinds.
- The object the story turns on gets an insert the first time it appears. The answer gets a close-up or an insert.

On-screen text:

- `onScreenText` is a few words laid over the drawing, six at most. Use it for what a drawing can't show: a name, a time or a day, a number, how two people are related. It is not a transcript of the line.
- Change it only when there is something new to say. Never repeat the previous shot's text.
- `onScreen` says where the viewer sees what the line claims: in the drawing ("wet footprints on the hallway floor") or in the text ("on-screen text: 3:07 a.m.").

Sound:

- A quiet music bed that suits the mood, plus a sound that belongs to the action where the action makes one: the key in the lock, the hum of the fridge. Nothing competes with the voice.
- Drop to near silence in the shot before the answer.

The caption:

- It opens with the situation and the question. It may hold the answer back, but nothing in it contradicts how the story ends.
- It ends with a vote: a choice the character faced, put to the viewer, answerable in a word. "Would you have opened it? Tell me in the comments."
- Then 3 to 5 hashtags, about the story and the kind of video, none naming a product or the business.

Three checks for this format before you answer:

1. Read only the lines. Do they pass the ears-only test?
2. Read each `visual` alone, as the artist will. Could you draw it without the others, and tell who is who?
3. Is every shot one instant that a still drawing can hold?

A moody flat-vector film still, like the best stick-figure story channels. It is one shot of a short vertical video watched on a phone, on screen for two or three seconds, so it has to read at a glance: who is here, where they are, and the one thing that is happening.

Draw exactly what the shot's `visual` says, framed the way its `camera` says. One action, caught mid-move. Add nothing that changes what the moment means.

Characters:

- White stick figures: stroke #f5f5f5, stroke-width 12 to 18, round caps and joins. Use the thicker end of the range when the figure is large in the frame, and the same width for every figure in one frame.
- A round head, a spine, arms that bend at the elbow and legs that bend at the knee, so the pose itself shows the action: leaning, reaching, recoiling, crouching. A figure stands straight with its arms down only when the visual says it is frozen.
- A simple expressive face: eyes, brows and a mouth that show the feeling the visual names. It should read from across a room.
- When the visual gives a character a marker (a cap, a ponytail, a scarf, a child's height), draw it clearly. It is how the viewer tells people apart from shot to shot.
- At most two figures. Whatever a figure holds is drawn at its hand, large enough to recognise.

The place:

- Drawn in full, edge to edge, with depth: a floor or ground plane in perspective, walls or sky behind, and 2 or 3 layers where far things are smaller and darker.
- Real detail that says where we are (bricks, a fence, a tree, a doorway, a car), plus every object the visual names, each recognisable from its outline.
- Words appear in the drawing only when the visual quotes them. Draw them large and exactly as written.

Light:

- Light comes from something in the scene (a lamp, a window, the sun, a streetlight), drawn as soft radial gradients, with a pool of light on the ground and a soft shadow under each character.
- Everything else falls off into dark (#0e1117 to #3a4150).
- The lit area is where the action is. The viewer's eye should land on it first.

Colour:

- One accent, lime #a3e635, used small, for what matters (the key prop, a glow). Red #ef4444 only for danger. Nothing big and flat lime.

Framing, by camera:

- wide: the whole place. Figures are a quarter to a third of the frame's height.
- medium: figures from the knees up, filling about two thirds of the height.
- close-up: head and shoulders. The face is the picture.
- over-the-shoulder: the back of one figure's head and shoulder, large at one side of the foreground, with the other figure facing us beyond it.
- low angle: the horizon is low and we look up, so the subject towers.
- high angle: we look down, the ground fills most of the frame, and the figure is small.
- insert: the object fills most of the frame, with at most a hand beside it.
