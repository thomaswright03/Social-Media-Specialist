# Format: product ad (20% of Reels)

One product from wright-ai-solutions.com, advertised to the people who would
use it. Thomas, 2026-11-20, after the first test ad:

1. "The ad is for individuals regulating their daily consumption and no one is
   gonna eat straight ice cream": the ad speaks to the product's real users,
   in situations they really live.
2. "Ads should not have stickmen in them they should only be like the
   informational entertainment ads with slides and transitions but it will
   inform people about the app": the explainer's motion graphics, plus the
   product's own screens (the motion kit's `app` piece).
3. "We are advertising people to use the calorie app. not offering to make
   apps": the ad sells the product, never custom software (code checks this).

Format: an ad. It advertises ONE product (below) to the people who would use it. It never offers to build software, apps or AI agents for anyone.

Who it's for: read the product's description below and decide who really uses it. A calorie and weight tracker is for an ordinary person keeping an eye on what they eat each day, not a business. A lead finder is for the company that buys it. Pitch to that person, about their normal day.

What you are pitching: a short, useful explainer, about 35 seconds, told with bold slides and transitions and the product's own screens, that informs the viewer about the product. It has to entertain first: a viewer who will never use it should still enjoy it, because a viewer who scrolls past never reaches the product. The pitches that win share a shape:
- One everyday question the viewer already has ("Do you actually know what you ate today?"), in their words.
- One real moment where it bites, the way it really happens (a normal lunch, a busy weekday, the evening snack), never something silly or extreme.
- The product answering it with one or two real features, shown on its screen.
- What is easier for the viewer afterwards.

A pitch's `sentence` works like any other stage 2 pitch: one sentence that sets up one everyday moment the viewer knows and leaves one specific thing open, without giving the ending away. It is not a feature list and not a description of the product working (cold readers skip those). Its `answer` is how the product settles it, with one real feature, naming the product by its exact name. Example, not to reuse: sentence "You stayed under your calories all week, so why did the scale go the other way?"; answer "Free Calorie Counter's daily log shows the weekend snacks you never wrote down."

Test every pitch before you offer it:
1. Real-life test. This is how the product's real users live and use it. Nobody in it does something no real person would do (no one eats a tub of ice cream for lunch). If it only works as a joke, skip it.
2. Recognise test. Someone the product is for recognises the question at once.
3. Feature test. One or two real features of this product, from what is given below, do the work.
4. Honesty test. Nothing in the pitch is something the product can't do, and no result is claimed that the product's page doesn't claim.
5. Sells-the-product test. It invites the viewer to use the product. It never offers to build an app, software or AI agents.

Pitches that fail:
- A list of every feature.
- A made-up person whose story the ad is about. The ad speaks to the viewer ("you").
- A made-up customer praising the product. That is an invented review.
- Anything about businesses, when the product is for individuals.

Format: an ad. It advertises ONE product (below) to the people who would use it. Name no other product. Never offer to build software, apps or AI agents for anyone (code checks this).

It has to entertain first. A viewer who will never use the product should still enjoy it, because a viewer who scrolls past never reaches the product.

How this video is made, which is the reason for the rules below: one narrator's voice plays over a sequence of bold motion graphics, one per shot, with transitions between them. Some shots are the product's own screen on a phone. Each graphic is made by a designer who may see that shot alone. There are no characters and no stick figures.

The examples in these rules show the kind of thing meant. Do not reuse them.

Who it's for:
- Decide from the product's description who really uses it, and speak to that person as "you". A calorie counter speaks to someone watching what they eat, not to a shop owner.
- Everything happens the way real life does: normal meals, normal days, normal amounts. Nothing silly, extreme or impossible (nobody eats a tub of ice cream for lunch).

Honesty. Breaking one of these makes the ad false:
- Only claims the product's page makes. A number about the product (time saved, a price) is said only if the page says it, and exactly as the page says it.
- On screen, the product does only what it really does, with the words a real screen of it would show.
- Example entries on its screen (a meal and its calories, a weight) are ordinary and realistic, and are shown as an example of what you'd log, never as a fact about food or health.
- No reviews, no testimonials, no customer quotes, no "#1", no guarantees, no health or weight-loss promises.

The shape, in this order:
1. The viewer's everyday question, in the first 3 seconds, in plain words. This is the opening.
2. Why it's hard: the real moment it bites, said to "you".
3. The product arrives, and the narrator names it.
4. The product doing its real features, on its screen, in 2 or 3 shots, each showing something different.
5. What is easier for you now. This is the `payoff`.
6. The last shot is the call to action: what it costs if the page says (free), and "link in bio".

The narrator:
- A friendly, confident explainer talking to the viewer.
- Speaks from the first second (the first shot has a line) and in every shot.
- Says the product's name when it first appears. Says Wright AI Solutions at most once, only as who made it, never as an offer.
- Short plain words, short sentences, no jargon. Every line follows from the one before.
- Ears-only test, checked by code: listeners who hear only the narration must say what it's about and what the product does.

The graphics (`visual`):
- Each shot shows ONE graphic that proves its line: a big number counting up, a before-and-after compare, a short list ticking in, an icon of the thing (a plate, a phone, a clock), a calendar day, a goal meter.
- Every `visual` stands on its own. Say what kind of graphic it is, its exact values and labels, and which one part is highlighted.

The product's screen:
- 2 or 3 shots are the product's screen on a phone, each showing a different moment of the feature working (logging something, the goal filling, the progress over days).
- Give the exact words on the screen in `visual`, in quotes: the screen's name, at most 3 short rows of a label and a value ("Oatmeal: 320 cal"), and, if it has one, the goal with its number ("1,240 of 1,800 cal") and one button ("+ Add food").
- Show the feature finishing its job, not a menu or a logo.

Numbers and text:
- Every number is on screen as it is said.
- `onScreenText` carries the few words that say what the shot is about. Never put the same words in both `visual` and `onScreenText`.

The camera, used as graphic moves:
- `camera` is exactly one of: wide, medium, close-up, over-the-shoulder, low angle, high angle, insert.
- The product's screen is always an insert. close-up: one number. wide: a whole list or compare.
- Change the camera on every cut, and use at least three kinds.

Other fields:
- `place` is the graphic space ("the slides", "the phone"). One or two in the whole video.
- `story` says in two sentences what the product does for the viewer and how.

The caption:
- It names the product, says in one line what it does for you, and ends with "Link in bio", then 3 to 5 hashtags its real users follow (for a calorie counter: #calorietracking, not #smallbusiness).

Bold, friendly motion graphics, like a sharp product explainer. Each shot is watched on a phone for two or three seconds, so each graphic makes one point and makes it huge. No characters and no stick figures.

Background:
- Deep navy to black (#0b1020 to #121a33) with a faint grid, edge to edge.

Graphics:
- Big flat shapes, thick clean lines, and simple icons built from geometry.
- Depth comes from layered cards with soft drop shadows, not from perspective scenes.

The product's screen:
- A light phone screen with the product's name at the top, its rows and goal drawn big and readable, labels at least 36 px tall, with the words exactly as the shot gives them.
- One thing on the screen is the point. It is highlighted in lime.

Numbers and labels:
- Drawn huge in a heavy sans-serif (generic sans-serif, weight 900, no external fonts), with the key number at least 120 px tall.
- Inside x 80 to 940 and between y 340 and y 1250.

Palette, 2 or 3 colours in any one graphic:
- White for the base.
- Lime #a3e635 for the product's moment of success and for its buttons.
- Red #ef4444 only for the problem.
- Muted blue-grey #64748b for everything else.

Motion:
- Things slide in, count up, fill and highlight, each on a crisp ease-out.
- The highlighted part moves last, so the eye ends on it.
